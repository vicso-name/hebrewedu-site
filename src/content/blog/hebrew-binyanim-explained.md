There is a specific moment Hebrew learners dread.

Things are going reasonably well. The alphabet — manageable. Basic vocabulary — building. Simple sentences — possible.

Then someone mentions:

> "Oh, and there are seven verb patterns. They're called Binyanim."

And suddenly the room temperature drops several degrees.

Seven verb patterns.

**Hebrew binyanim** (בִּנְיָנִים, “buildings”; singular binyan, בִּנְיָן) are the seven verb patterns of Hebrew: Pa'al, Nif'al, Pi'el, Pu'al, Hif'il, Huf'al and Hitpa'el. Every Hebrew verb is a root of (usually) three letters set into one of these patterns. The root carries the meaning; the binyan sets the shape of the verb and shifts its meaning: simple, intensive, causative, reflexive or passive.

It is one of those terms that makes Hebrew grammar sound considerably more terrifying than it actually is.

Here's the thing: you don't need to master all seven at once. You don't need all seven to hold a real conversation. And the patterns, once you see them working in actual words, are more logical than they first appear.

---

## What a Binyan Is

A Binyan is a verb pattern — a structural template that determines the form and modifies the meaning of a Hebrew root.

Hebrew verbs are not random. Every Hebrew verb belongs to one of seven Binyanim. Each Binyan follows a predictable shape across all the verbs within it.

The same root can appear in multiple Binyanim. Each time it does, its meaning shifts in a systematic, recognizable way.

An example will make this concrete immediately.

---

## One Root, Multiple Binyanim

Take the root כ-ת-ב (k-t-v), which carries the meaning of writing. Here's how the same root looks across four different Binyanim:

| Binyan | Verb | Transliteration | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pa'al | כָּתַב | katav | he wrote |
| Nif'al | נִכְתַּב | nikhtav | was written |
| Hif'il | הִכְתִּיב | hikhtiv | he dictated |
| Hitpa'el | הִתְכַּתֵּב | hitkatev | he corresponded |

The root stays constant: כ-ת-ב.

The pattern around it changes — and so does the meaning. Passive, causative, reciprocal — each Binyan adds a distinct semantic layer to the same core meaning.

Once you start seeing this, it changes how you look at every Hebrew verb you encounter.

## The 7 Hebrew Binyanim at a Glance

| Binyan | What it does | Example verb |
|---|---|---|
| **Pa'al** (פָּעַל) | Basic action — the most common | [לִכְתֹּב *likhtov*, to write](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-likhtov) |
| **Nif'al** (נִפְעַל) | Passive of Pa'al, plus many “happening” verbs | [לְהִכָּנֵס *lehikanes*, to enter](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehikanes) |
| **Pi'el** (פִּעֵל) | Intensive or extended action | [לְדַבֵּר *ledaber*, to speak](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-ledaber) |
| **Pu'al** (פֻּעַל) | Passive of Pi'el | [דֻּבַּר *dubar*, was spoken](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-dubar) |
| **Hif'il** (הִפְעִיל) | Causative — making something happen | [לְהַזְמִין *lehazmin*, to invite, to order](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehazmin) |
| **Huf'al** (הֻפְעַל) | Passive of Hif'il | [הוּבַן *huvan*, was understood](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-huvan) |
| **Hitpa'el** (הִתְפַּעֵל) | Reflexive or reciprocal | [לְהִתְקַשֵּׁר *lehitkasher*, to call](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehitkasher) |

Don't memorize this table right now.

What's worth noticing is the structure. Three of the seven are passive forms — they mirror active Binyanim. One is reflexive. Two carry most of the active verbs you'll encounter in everyday Hebrew: Pa'al and Pi'el.

## Start Here: Pa'al

Pa'al is the foundation.

It contains the most frequent verbs in spoken and written Hebrew. The most common everyday words live here:

- לִכְתֹּב (*likhtov*) — to write
- לֶאֱכֹל (*le'ekhol*) — to eat
- לָלֶכֶת (*lalekhet*) — to go/walk
- לִקְרֹא (*likro*) — to read
- לִרְאוֹת (*lir'ot*) — to see
- לִשְׁמֹעַ (*lishmoa*) — to hear

If you're at the beginning, Pa'al verbs are the primary [verb vocabulary you need](/blog/how-many-words-are-in-the-modern-hebrew-language/) at every level. Most everyday sentences function entirely within Pa'al.

Learn Pa'al first. Get comfortable with it. Let the other six emerge in due course.

---

## The Second One Worth Learning Early: Pi'el

Pi'el is the second most common Binyan in everyday Hebrew.

It often adds intensity, repetition, or a causative element to the action.

With the root ל-מ-ד (l-m-d — relating to learning):
- Pa'al: לָמַד (*lamad*) — he learned
- Pi'el: לִמֵּד (*limed*) — he taught (causing someone to learn)

With the root ד-ב-ר (d-b-r — speaking):
- Pi'el: לְדַבֵּר (*ledaber*) — to speak; דִּבֵּר (*diber*) — he spoke

The Pa'al / Pi'el relationship often follows this pattern: Pa'al is the simpler action, Pi'el introduces a more deliberate or intensive version. Learning to recognize both doubles the verbs you can understand for every root you know.

Pi'el is also easy to hear: the present tense starts with מְ (*me-*) — מְדַבֵּר (*medaber*), מְלַמֵּד (*melamed*), מְשַׁלֵּם (*meshalem*, pays).

## The Other Five Binyanim

You don't need these on day one. But each has a recognizable shape and a job, and knowing the job makes new verbs far easier to guess.

### Nif'al (נִפְעַל): passive, and things that happen

Nif'al is the passive of Pa'al: כָּתַב (*katav*, he wrote) becomes נִכְתַּב (*nikhtav*, was written).

In practice, many Nif'al verbs are not passive in English at all. They describe things that happen to you or that you do without an object:

- [לְהִכָּנֵס (*lehikanes*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehikanes) — to enter
- [לְהִפָּגֵשׁ (*lehipagesh*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehipagesh) — to meet
- [לְהִשָּׁאֵר (*lehisha'er*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehishaer) — to stay

How to spot it: נִ (*ni-*) at the start in the past and present (נִכְנַס, *nikhnas*, he entered), and לְהִ (*lehi-*) with a dagesh in the first root letter in the infinitive (לְהִכָּנֵס).

### Hif'il (הִפְעִיל): making something happen

Hif'il is the causative binyan: it often means causing someone or something to do the Pa'al action. With כ-ת-ב, הִכְתִּיב (*hikhtiv*) is “he dictated”, he made someone write.

It also holds plenty of everyday verbs that have no Pa'al partner you need to know:

- [לְהַתְחִיל (*lehatkhil*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehatkhil) — to start
- [לְהַסְבִּיר (*lehasbir*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehasbir) — to explain
- [לְהָבִין (*lehavin*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehavin) — to understand
- [לְהַגִּיעַ (*lehagia*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehagia) — to arrive

How to spot it: הִ…ִי… in the past (הִזְמִין, *hizmin*, he invited), מַ (*ma-*) in the present (מַזְמִין, *mazmin*), and לְהַ (*leha-*) in the infinitive.

### Hitpa'el (הִתְפַּעֵל): doing it to yourself, or to each other

Hitpa'el is reflexive or reciprocal: the action turns back on the subject. הִתְכַּתֵּב (*hitkatev*) is “corresponded”, wrote to each other.

- [לְהִתְקַשֵּׁר (*lehitkasher*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehitkasher) — to call (someone)
- [לְהִתְאַמֵּן (*lehit'amen*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehitamen) — to train, to practice
- [לְהִשְׁתַּמֵּשׁ (*lehishtamesh*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehishtamesh) — to use
- [לְהִסְתַּכֵּל (*lehistakel*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-lehistakel) — to look

How to spot it: הִתְ (*hit-*) in the past, מִתְ (*mit-*) in the present, לְהִתְ (*lehit-*) in the infinitive.

One twist: when the root starts with ס or שׁ, the ת swaps places with that letter. That's why it's הִשְׁתַּמֵּשׁ (*hishtamesh*) and הִסְתַּכֵּל (*histakel*), not “hitshamesh” or “hitsakel”.

### Pu'al (פֻּעַל) and Huf'al (הֻפְעַל): the strict passives

Pu'al is the passive of Pi'el; Huf'al is the passive of Hif'il. Both carry an *u* vowel: דֻּבַּר (*dubar*, was spoken), הוּבַן (*huvan*, was understood), הֻחְלַט (*hukhlat*, was decided).

They have no infinitive and no imperative, so you'll meet them less in conversation. Where they do show up constantly is in the present tense, used as everyday adjectives:

- [מוּבָן (*muvan*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-huvan) — understood, clear (כַּמּוּבָן, *kamuvan*, “of course”)
- [מְשֻׁנֶּה (*meshune*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-shuna) — strange, odd
- [מְנֻסֶּה (*menuse*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-nusa) — experienced
- [מְדֻבָּר (*medubar*)](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#v-dubar) — spoken of: בַּמֶּה מְדֻבָּר? (*bame medubar?*) — “what is it about?”

## How to Tell Which Binyan a Verb Is In

Grammars show the patterns with the model root פ-ע-ל (“to act”), which is where the binyan names come from. Put any root in place of פ, ע and ל and you get the real verb.

| Binyan | Past (he) | Present (he) | Infinitive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pa'al | פָּעַל | פּוֹעֵל | לִפְעֹל |
| Nif'al | נִפְעַל | נִפְעָל | לְהִפָּעֵל |
| Pi'el | פִּעֵל | מְפַעֵל | לְפַעֵל |
| Pu'al | פֻּעַל | מְפֻעָל | — |
| Hif'il | הִפְעִיל | מַפְעִיל | לְהַפְעִיל |
| Huf'al | הֻפְעַל | מֻפְעָל | — |
| Hitpa'el | הִתְפַּעֵל | מִתְפַּעֵל | לְהִתְפַּעֵל |

The giveaways to look for:

- **Pa'al:** *o*–*e* in the present — כּוֹתֵב (*kotev*)
- **Nif'al:** נִ (*ni-*) at the start
- **Pi'el:** מְ (*me-*) in the present and a dagesh in the middle letter
- **Pu'al:** a *u* vowel after the first letter, מְ in the present
- **Hif'il:** הִ…ִי in the past, מַ (*ma-*) in the present
- **Huf'al:** הֻ / מֻ (*hu-* / *mu-*)
- **Hitpa'el:** הִתְ / מִתְ (*hit-* / *mit-*)

Real roots bend these shapes: a guttural letter, a ו or י in the root, or a root ending in ה changes the vowels. לְהָבִין (*lehavin*) is Hif'il even though it doesn't match לְהַפְעִיל letter for letter. The giveaways still work most of the time, and they get you to the right table fast.

## How This Connects to the Root System

Binyanim don't make sense in isolation.

They are inseparable from [the Hebrew root system](/blog/how-the-hebrew-root-system-works/). Each Binyan is a pattern applied to a root. Understanding roots lets you:

- Recognize which Binyan a verb belongs to
- Predict the meaning shift when a root appears in a different Binyan
- Make reasonable guesses about unfamiliar verbs when you encounter them

This is why root knowledge and Binyan recognition are learned together, not separately. They are two parts of the same system.

---

## The Mistake Beginners Make With Binyanim

Trying to learn the system before the vocabulary.

Binyanim are a useful framework. They are not what creates fluency.

Real facility with Hebrew verbs comes from encountering them repeatedly in context — reading, listening, speaking — until the patterns become intuitive rather than analytical.

This is one of the [grammar mistakes that consistently slow beginners down](/blog/how-not-to-learn-hebrew-a-complete-guide-to-failing-efficiently/): treating Binyanim as something to master from a table before actually using verbs in real sentences.

Tables are reference material. They describe a system.

They are not how the brain acquires language.

---

## How to Approach Binyanim Practically

Rather than drilling all seven patterns from the start, a more effective approach:

1. **Learn verbs as vocabulary first** — full infinitive, meaning, and one or two example sentences
2. **Notice which Binyan each verb belongs to** as you encounter it — don't force it, just notice
3. **Start recognizing the Pa'al shape** across the verbs you know
4. **Add Pi'el awareness** once Pa'al feels automatic
5. **Let Hif'il, Hitpa'el, and the passives emerge** through exposure over months — not weeks

When you want to check a form, look it up instead of guessing: the [Hebrew verb conjugation tables](/learn/hebrew-verbs/#verbs) on this site cover 30 common verbs across all seven binyanim. ## What Binyanim Actually Give You

Once Pa'al and Pi'el become familiar, something useful starts happening.

You encounter an unfamiliar verb. You don't know its meaning. But you recognize:
- the root (which you do know)
- the Binyan (which you've started to feel)

And you can make an educated guess.

Not perfectly.

But close enough to continue reading or listening without stopping.

That ability — to keep moving through Hebrew rather than halting at every unknown word — is one of the most important skills in language acquisition. Binyanim are part of what makes it possible.

---

## Final Thought

Seven Binyanim sounds like a lot.

Two of them — Pa'al and Pi'el — cover the overwhelming majority of verbs you will encounter in your first year.

Three of the remaining five are passive forms. They matter, but they're not urgent.

The other two you will absorb gradually, through exposure, until they start feeling like patterns you recognize rather than rules you applied.

Binyanim are not seven obstacles.

They are seven tools.

And you only need to pick up two of them at the start.
