And then, slowly, it clicks.

That click is what this guide is about. By the end, you'll understand not just what the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet are, but why they look the way they do, how they sound, and — most importantly — how to actually start reading them.

## Why the Hebrew Alphabet Is Easier Than It Looks

Most people assume Hebrew will be hard before they've even tried. The script looks ancient, exotic, impenetrable. Hebrew has 22 letters and no upper or lower case. In standard Modern Hebrew, some letters may have little or no consonant sound in particular words, while vowel marks help show how to read a word.

Compare that to English, with its 26 letters and many spelling-pronunciation exceptions. Hebrew's letter-to-sound patterns are more regular in some ways, though pronunciation depends on the letter, its marks and the variety of Hebrew being described.

The main challenges are:
- It reads **right to left**
- Most everyday text is written **without vowel markings**
- Five letters have different forms when they appear at the end of a word

That's genuinely the hard part. The rest is just practice.

The chart lists 22 base letters. Five of those letters also have a distinct form used at the end of a word; these are variants, not five additional base letters.

Dalet (ד) is a good example of how a letter's name carries its own meaning: *dalet* means "door" in Hebrew, and tradition holds that the letter's shape echoes one.

→ [Practice Dalet in the Hebrew Alphabet Course](/learn/hebrew-alphabet/dalet/)

Hey (ה) does double duty: beyond being the fifth letter, it's also the definite article "the" at the start of a word (הַ-), and it's often silent when it ends a word.

→ [Practice Hey in the Hebrew Alphabet Course](/learn/hebrew-alphabet/he/)

Vav (ו) is doubly useful: as a consonant it makes the [v] sound, but it also stands in for two vowels — Holam Male for [o] and Shuruk for [u] — and as a prefix (וְ-) it means "and."

→ [Practice Vav in the Hebrew Alphabet Course](/learn/hebrew-alphabet/vav/)

Zayin (ז) is one of the more straightforward letters: it consistently represents the [z] sound, as in the English word "zebra," with no alternate pronunciation to track.

→ [Practice Zayin in the Hebrew Alphabet Course](/learn/hebrew-alphabet/zayin/)

Chet (ח) is often the first sound English speakers have to practise: in modern Hebrew it sounds like the ch in German "Bach," not the ch in English "chair." At the end of a word, a Patah under it is read before the letter, as in כּוֹחַ [ko-ach], "strength."

→ [Practice Chet in the Hebrew Alphabet Course](/learn/hebrew-alphabet/chet/)

Tet (ט) is a plain [t], as in "too," and in standard Modern Hebrew it sounds exactly like Tav (ת), so you learn which of the two a word uses from its spelling. It begins טוֹב [tov], "good," as in the greeting "boker tov."

→ [Practice Tet in the Hebrew Alphabet Course](/learn/hebrew-alphabet/tet/)

Yod (י), also spelled Yud, is one of the smallest letters and does two jobs: as a consonant it is [y], as in "yes," in יָד [yad], "hand"; after a Hiriq it helps spell the vowel [i], as in בִּי [bi]. It looks like a short Vav (ו) that stops halfway up the line.

→ [Practice Yod in the Hebrew Alphabet Course](/learn/hebrew-alphabet/yod/)

Kaf (כ) has two sounds: with a dagesh, כּ is [k], as in "kite"; without it, כ is [kh] (often called Khaf or Chaf), as in כּוֹכָב [kokhav], "star". It is also the first of the five letters with a final form, ך, written only at the end of a word.

→ [Practice Kaf in the Hebrew Alphabet Course](/learn/hebrew-alphabet/kaf/)

Lamed (ל) is a plain [l], as in "leaf," in לֵב [lev], "heart." It is the tallest Hebrew letter, its top stroke rising above the line, and at the start of a word it can be a prefix meaning "to" or "for," as in לְדָוִד [le-da-vid], "to David."

→ [Practice Lamed in the Hebrew Alphabet Course](/learn/hebrew-alphabet/lamed/)

## The Five Final Letters

Five Hebrew letters have a different written form when they appear at the **end of a word**. These are called *sofit* forms (סופית — “final”). The shape change is different for each pair.

The five pairs are:
- **Kaf → Chaf sofit** (כ → ך)
- **Mem → Mem sofit** (מ → ם)
- **Nun → Nun sofit** (נ → ן)
- **Pe → Fe sofit** (פ → ף)
- **Tzadi → Tzadi sofit** (צ → ץ)

You can see them in familiar words: **מֶלֶךְ** (<bdi lang="he" dir="rtl">מלך</bdi>, *melekh*, “king”) ends with final Kaf; **שָׁלוֹם** (<bdi lang="he" dir="rtl">שלום</bdi>, *shalom*, “peace”) ends with final Mem; and **קָטָן** (<bdi lang="he" dir="rtl">קטן</bdi>, *katan*, “small” in the masculine form) ends with final Nun.

Once you recognize these, they become second nature. You'll start to see word boundaries more clearly, which makes reading faster.

## The Vowel System: Nikud

Classical Hebrew texts — the Torah, liturgical writings, poetry — are written with a system of dots and dashes placed around the letters called **Nikud** (נִקּוּד). These vowel markings tell you exactly how to pronounce each word.

Modern Hebrew, as printed in newspapers, books, and on street signs, almost never uses Nikud. Native speakers fill in the vowels from context and vocabulary knowledge.

For beginners, this is both a challenge and a relief. Start with Nikud — it gives you training wheels. Once you can read Hebrew words correctly, you gradually learn to read without them. Understanding [why everyday Hebrew removes those vowel markings](/blog/why-hebrew-without-vowels-looks-impossible-until-your-brain-adapts/) — and how native readers reconstruct them automatically — is one of the most useful things to grasp early on. For a step-by-step explanation of the marks, continue to the [Nikud Guide](/blog/how-to-read-hebrew-nikud/).

> The Hebrew Aleph Bet app includes Nikud from the very first lesson. Every letter is introduced with proper vowel markings so you build correct pronunciation habits from day one.

## Two-Sound Letters: Bet/Vet and Shin/Sin

A few Hebrew letters represent **two different sounds** depending on context. The most important ones are:

**ב (Bet/Vet)**
- With a dot inside (בּ) → sounds like **B** (as in "boat")
- Without the dot (ב) → sounds like **V** (as in "vine")
- → [Practice Bet/Vet in the Hebrew Alphabet Course](/learn/hebrew-alphabet/bet/)

**ש (Shin/Sin)**
- Dot on the right (שׁ) → sounds like **SH** (as in "shoe")
- Dot on the left (שׂ) → sounds like **S** (as in "sun")

In standard Modern Hebrew, Tet (ט) and Tav (ת) are both pronounced /t/. Kaf (כ) and Kuf (ק) need one more distinction: Kaf with dagesh, כּ, is /k/; Kaf without dagesh, כ or ך, is /χ/, commonly approximated in English as “kh” or “ch”; Kuf, ק, is /k/. The dot (dagesh) changes the Kaf sound.

## How Hebrew Reading Actually Works

Once you know the letters and the vowel system, Hebrew reading follows a simple pattern:

Read the Hebrew letters from right to left, using the vowel marks to work out the sounds.

So the word שָׁלוֹם (shalom — peace) breaks down as:
- שׁ (sh) + ָ (a) = sha
- ל (l) + וֹ (o) = lo
- ם (final Mem) = /m/ — pronounced at the end of the word, with no following vowel

Read together: **sha-lom**.

That's the basic process. Familiarity grows with practice, but the time it takes to sound out words varies by learner and by how much they practice.

## Three Common Mistakes Beginners Make

**1. Trying to memorize all 22 letters at once**

Don't. Learn five or six letters, practice reading simple syllables with them, then add more. Your brain retains patterns much better than it retains isolated symbols. The Hebrew Alphabet app introduces letters progressively for exactly this reason.

**2. Skipping audio**

Hebrew has sounds that don't exist in English — the guttural Chet (ח) and Ayin (ע) in particular. If you learn the letters only visually without hearing them, you'll build bad pronunciation habits that are hard to unlearn. Always learn with audio from the start.

**3. Treating the script as decoration**

Some beginners spend too long admiring the aesthetic of Hebrew letters without actually drilling recognition. The goal is to see ג and immediately think "Gimel, G" without a pause. That automatic recognition only comes from active practice — writing, typing, sounding out syllables — not passive reading. Writing the letters is also good preparation for later, when you'll discover that [Hebrew handwriting looks completely different from the printed forms](/blog/why-hebrew-handwriting-looks-completely-different-from-printed-hebrew/) you've been learning.

## How Long Does It Actually Take?

There is no single timeline for learning to read basic Hebrew. Progress depends on prior experience, practice time and what “read” means—from recognizing letters to decoding unfamiliar words fluently.

Reading, not understanding. Those are different goals. Some learners find they can sound out words while still understanding little of what they're reading. [Decoding and comprehension](/blog/why-you-can-read-hebrew-but-still-understand-absolutely-nothing/) are different skills, and progress in each varies.

Regular practice with letters, syllables and simple texts can build familiarity. The time needed to recognize letters, decode new words and read fluently varies by learner.

The second phase — understanding what you're reading — takes longer. But it starts with the alphabet, and the alphabet is very much learnable.
